\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
To assess the effectiveness of TinyNet, we employ our hardware testbed, focusing on accurately timing key message passing intervals and associated delays. These empirical measurements serve to calibrate and inform our simulation model. The simulation, in turn, facilitates rapid iteration across a variety of network traffic scenarios. It enables us to meticulously analyze and quantify the routing paths of messages within the network, offering a detailed insight into TinyNet's performance under different conditions. This combined approach of practical testing and simulation provides a comprehensive evaluation of TinyNet's capabilities.

\subsection{Hardware}
For our hardware evaluation, we utilized the Saleae Logic 8 Logic Analyzer to capture logic level voltages from the microcontrollers. This was crucial for monitoring the router firmware at both the application and networking layers, where pin states (high or low) are indicative of significant event occurrences.

\subsubsection{Processing Delay}
\begin{figure*}[th]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/results-D_p.png}
    \caption{Logic-analyzer capture showing $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$ measurement. Channels show UART activity: Channel 1 = incoming packet end, Channel 2 = outgoing packet start; the interval between them is $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}} = 37\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Channel 3 shows per-byte interrupt handling ($\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{byte}} = 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$).}
    \label{fig:packet-delay}
\end{figure*}
Processing Delay, denoted $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$, is defined as the duration required for a router to determine the appropriate port for forwarding a received packet. To characterize this delay, we recorded signals at the link layer using the logic analyzer, tracking a packet's journey through a single router. Specifically, $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$ was measured as the interval between the end of an incoming packet and the start of the outgoing packet's transmission. By averaging results from 10 such packet traversals, we determined an average processing delay of $37 \mu\mathrm{s}$. The small sample size is justified by the deterministic nature of the MCU's execution path: the forwarding logic executes the same instruction sequence for every packet and runs at the exclusive priority of the main loop, with no competing interrupts during processing. Accordingly, trial-to-trial variation is negligible and the 10-sample mean is representative.

\subsubsection{Link Transmission Time}
Link Transmission Time, defined in equation~(\ref{eq:ltt}) as $\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{tt}}$, is the time taken for a packet to travel across a link. This calculation was verified using a logic analyzer. The transmission and reception of bytes also consume processor time. As the UART link handles bit-shifting asynchronously on each port, each received character triggers an interrupt requiring processor attention. We measured this interrupt handling time to be $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{byte}}=1.5 \mu \mathrm{s}$ per byte, consistent across all tested packets. This value can also be derived analytically: at 300 MHz with the interrupt service routine requiring approximately 450 clock cycles for context save, buffer copy, and return, the expected handling time is $450/300\,\mathrm{MHz} = 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, confirming the measured result. This process is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:packet-delay}, Channel 3. We discuss in Section~\ref{sec:sim-val} how we account for these delays to align our simulation results with our hardware findings.

\subsection{Simulation Validation}
\label{sec:sim-val}
Our simulation, capable of representing various network topologies and traffic conditions, operates in JavaScript and leverages the open-source Simbit architecture \cite{simbit}. To validate the simulation's effectiveness, we emulate a 12-node grid network, mirroring our hardware setup. We initialize the simulated nodes with parameters derived from hardware measurements via the logic analyzer: processing delay $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}} = 37\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, per-byte interrupt time $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{byte}} = 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and link bitrate $\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{br}} = 20\,\mathrm{MHz}$. All simulations use $\lambda = 0.5$, a heartbeat transmission period of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, and a missed-heartbeat detection window of $2\,\mathrm{ms}$. This process ensures that our simulation closely resembles real-world hardware operations.

We facilitate message transmission diagonally across the grid and record the round-trip time (RTT). Comparing the RTT of $511\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ obtained from our hardware tests with the simulation's RTT of $505\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, we observe a minor discrepancy of only $1.2\%$, confirming that the simulation accurately reproduces hardware behavior at this operating point. RTT variance and failure-recovery statistics reported in subsequent sections are derived from simulation; hardware validation of these statistics remains future work.

\subsection{Simulation Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\textcolor{red}{All six perturbations remain stable at $\pm15\%$, confirming that the simulation results are robust to plausible hardware timing uncertainty. Bitrate and per-byte interrupt time have a moderate influence: a $\pm15\%$ change in $\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{br}}$ changes $\sigma$ by $0\%$ to $+60\%$, and a $\pm15\%$ change in $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{byte}}$ by $+20\%$ to $+24\%$. $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$ is validated by two independent methods: direct logic-analyzer measurement ($37\,\mu\mathrm{s}$) and analytical derivation from the MCU's interrupt service routine (450~cycles $\div$ 300~MHz per-byte, with the forwarding path executing a deterministic instruction sequence). The 1.2\% RTT match at the hardware calibration point confirms this value, bounding the practical uncertainty in $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$ to well under 15\%. The parameters for which the analysis shows benign sensitivity, $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{byte}}$ and $\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{br}}$, are less tightly constrained by independent measurement but do not materially affect the reported results within their plausible uncertainty ranges.

\subsection{Determinism of Communication Time}
A crucial measure of TinyNet's effectiveness is communication time determinism, specifically the standard deviation of the round trip time for each hop a message makes. Different from previous systems that relied on spanning tree models, TinyNet thrives in more extensive network topologies. Therefore, our evaluation focuses on both determinism and robustness within a compact grid setting.

\subsubsection{Evaluation in a 4-by-4 Grid}
\begin{figure}[th]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pdf_grid_cross.png}
    \caption{\textcolor{red}{Per-hop RTT histograms in a 4$\times$4 grid in steady state ($t \geq 50\,\mathrm{ms}$ of a 200~ms simulation). Main flow: 5~kHz from node~0 to node~15. Cross-traffic on the opposite diagonal (node~3 to node~12) at 5~kHz (top) and 10~kHz (bottom).}}
    \label{fig:hists-cross}
\end{figure}
We begin by simulating a 4-by-4 grid of nodes, with each node having a packet delay time of $37 \mu \mathrm{s}$ (the hardware-measured $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{process}}$) and each link operating at a bitrate of 20 MHz. \textcolor{red}{We transmit crucial messages diagonally across the grid at 5 kHz while cross-traffic runs on the opposite diagonal at 5 or 10~kHz in separate simulations. Each simulation runs for 200~ms after a warm-up exchange, and all statistics are taken from $t \geq 50\,\mathrm{ms}$ so that they describe steady-state behavior rather than the start-up transient. The resulting per-hop RTT histograms are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hists-cross}. With 5-kHz cross-traffic, the mean per-hop RTT is $60\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ with $\sigma = 7\,\mu\mathrm{s}$; with 10-kHz cross-traffic, the mean is $104\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ with $\sigma = 25\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. If cross-traffic frequency increases beyond network capacity, no stable distribution exists.}

\subsubsection{Evaluation in a Simulated Airplane Wing Network}
\begin{figure}[th]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pdf_RTT.png}
    \caption{\textcolor{red}{Per-hop RTT histograms in a simulated airplane wing topology (one master node, three controllers, eight motors, and eight encoders) in steady state ($t \geq 50\,\mathrm{ms}$ of a 200~ms simulation), with traffic at 20\% of the nominal rates: master--encoder queries (left), controller--motor loops (center), and motor--encoder loops (right).}}
    \label{fig:hists-airplane}
\end{figure}
In addition, we assess determinism within a simulated airplane wing network, structured into four layers: a single master node on top, three controllers in the second layer, \textcolor{red}{each connected to the master and to all eight motors} in the third layer, and each motor linked to \textcolor{red}{its neighboring motors} and an encoder in the fourth layer. \textcolor{red}{The nominal traffic has each motor running a 500-Hz control loop with its encoder, each controller running a 200-Hz loop with every motor, and the master querying each encoder at 100~Hz. Using the steady-state method of the grid evaluation, the per-hop RTTs in Fig.~\ref{fig:hists-airplane} have a standard deviation of $29\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ for master--encoder queries, $66\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ for controller--motor loops, and under $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ for motor--encoder loops. Supporting the nominal rates would require either faster nodes or a topology that spreads controller traffic across more links.}

These results highlight two key outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Impact of Network Traffic on Determinism:} An increase in network traffic tends to reduce network determinism. As the number of packets traversing the network grows, so does the buffer depth at individual nodes, leading to increased message latency and variability in latency. This relationship is compounded by path length: networks with more routing hops expose each packet to more opportunities for queuing, so determinism degrades faster per unit of added traffic in larger networks. \textcolor{red}{This is consistent with the airplane wing results, where the single-hop motor--encoder loops achieve $\sigma < 1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ while the multi-hop master--encoder and controller--motor paths, which cross the heavily loaded controllers, exhibit $\sigma = 29$ and $66\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.}
    \item \textbf{TinyNet's Adaptability:} \textcolor{red}{Up to its capacity, TinyNet keeps per-hop RTT bounded under cross-traffic by distributing load across paths.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stateful Routing Baseline}
\label{sec:comparison}
}
To directly address the question of how TinyNet compares against stateful routing, we implemented a shortest-path baseline in a JavaScript simulator. The baseline pre-computes BFS shortest paths at startup and routes each packet along the single lowest-hop-count path, with no multipath. \textcolor{red}{Where several shortest paths exist, BFS takes the first in port order, which does not consider the traffic. We therefore also evaluate the baseline with \emph{planned routes}: for the traffic pattern of Section~\ref{sec:capacity}, we searched every combination of shortest paths for the four flow legs (two data flows and their acknowledgements) and selected the one that minimizes the peak processing load on any node. Planned routes represent a stateful network engineered in advance for known traffic.} \textcolor{red}{All comparisons use the same topology, traffic, timing parameters, and failure schedules for both protocols.}

\textcolor{red}{Failure handling in the baseline models Loop-Free Alternate (LFA) fast reroute~\cite{rfc5286,cisco_lfa} with Bidirectional Forwarding Detection (BFD)~\cite{rfc5880}. BFD detection time is the negotiated transmit interval multiplied by the detect multiplier, and vendor-documented lower limits on both vary widely. The most aggressive configuration we found documented is hardware-offloaded BFD at a 3.3-ms interval with a multiplier of 3 ($\approx\!10\,\mathrm{ms}$ detection), for which the vendor states a total convergence of under 50\,ms~\cite{cisco_ncs4200_bfd}. More common limits are far longer: a 50-ms minimum interval with a minimum multiplier of 3~\cite{juniper_bfd}, or recommended minimums of 100--300\,ms with a multiplier of 3~\cite{juniper_bfd}.} \textcolor{red}{We use two failure models. The \emph{cutover-window} model (Section~\ref{sec:robustness:single}) drops all packets on the failed path for a window $T_{\mathrm{LFA}}$ and then resumes on recomputed shortest paths, as if an alternate always existed. The \emph{coverage-aware} model (Section~\ref{sec:robustness:multi}) is more realistic: until BFD detects the failure 10\,ms after it occurs, neighbors keep forwarding into the dead links; at detection, each neighbor of a failed node switches affected destinations to an RFC~5286 alternate computed on the pre-failure topology (node-protecting where one exists, otherwise link-protecting), or drops them if no alternate exists; and all nodes recompute routes on the post-failure topology after an IGP reconvergence time of 200\,ms, the OSPF reconvergence figure cited in Section~\ref{sec:related:frr}. A 64-hop time-to-live discards packets caught in transient loops.} We compare against LFA rather than RPL because LFA represents the fastest-recovering class of stateful protocols in production use; RPL's DODAG repair mechanism targets energy-constrained wireless links and operates on a fundamentally different latency regime not directly applicable to UART-connected NCS (see Section~\ref{sec:related:mesh}).

\subsection{Traffic Capacity}
\label{sec:capacity}
\begin{figure*}[th]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/comparison_capacity.png}
    \caption{Delivery ratio (left) and mean per-hop RTT (right, log scale) on the 4$\times$4 grid versus offered load, as a multiple of a 5~kHz main flow (node~0 to node~15) plus 10~kHz cross-traffic (node~3 to node~12): TinyNet (blue circles), single-path routing with the default BFS tie-break (orange squares), and single-path routing with routes planned for this traffic (aqua triangles). Each point is a 200~ms simulation; statistics use $t \geq 50\,\mathrm{ms}$.}
    \label{fig:comparison-capacity}
\end{figure*}
}
\textcolor{red}{A comparison of RTT variability between protocols is meaningful only at loads where both are stable. We therefore compare capacity: the highest offered load at which a configuration delivers at least 99\% of packets and its mean RTT drifts by less than 20\% between the first and last 50~ms of the analysis window. Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison-capacity} shows delivery ratio and mean per-hop RTT as the load is scaled from 0.5 to 1.3 times the 5\,kHz plus 10\,kHz traffic of Fig.~\ref{fig:hists-cross}.}

\textcolor{red}{Single-path routing with the default tie-break is stable only up to 0.6 times this load. BFS sends both flows and their acknowledgements through the same column of nodes, overloading them; at 1.0 times the load, the baseline delivers 47\% of packets with a mean per-hop RTT of 3.6\,ms. By an analytic estimate of per-node processing load, 79\% of all combinations of shortest paths for this traffic overload at least one node, so this outcome is typical of single-path routing that does not account for the traffic. TinyNet is stable up to 1.15 times the load, about 1.9 times the capacity of the default single-path baseline, without any knowledge of the traffic. Single-path routing with planned routes is stable up to 1.0 times the load, comparable to TinyNet; we do not claim a difference between the two, because in both cases the busiest node is a flow endpoint, which no routing choice can relieve, and the remaining gap is within what small differences between the two simulator implementations could produce.}

\textcolor{red}{Below saturation, planned single-path routes deliver lower and more consistent per-hop RTT than TinyNet (e.g., $50 \pm 0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ versus $104 \pm 25\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ at 1.0 times the load), because periodic traffic on non-conflicting paths never queues, while TinyNet's cost function spreads packets over paths of varying length and occupancy. TinyNet's advantage is therefore not lower jitter than a network engineered for known traffic. It is that TinyNet reaches the capacity of such a network without planning, whereas single-path routing that is not planned for the traffic usually collapses well below it.}

\textcolor{red}{With a single failure, LFA often loses nothing: when the failed node is off a flow's path, or an RFC~5286 alternate covers it, delivery continues. In 18 of the 60 single-failure flows, however, no usable alternate existed, and the flow was dark until IGP reconvergence, losing up to 1{,}199 packets; TinyNet lost at most 19 packets in any single-failure flow. With two or more failures, the median LFA flow waits for reconvergence (about 600 packets lost for the 3-kHz flow and 1{,}200 for the 6-kHz flow), whereas TinyNet's median loss stays between 9 and 13 packets with a median blackout of 3--4\,ms. Comparing the same flow in the same trial, TinyNet lost fewer packets than LFA in 47\%, 75\%, 76\%, and 88\% of flows for $k = 1$ to 4, and more packets in 10\%, 7\%, 2\%, and 7\%. The LFA baseline's losses scale with the assumed 200\,ms reconvergence time; a faster-converging IGP would shrink, but not remove, this gap.}

\textcolor{red}{TinyNet failed to recover in 1 of the 213 flows, with four failures. In that trial, the failures isolated the destination of the other flow, whose source continued to send. Because TinyNet floods packets for which it has no route, these packets repeatedly flooded the surviving network, filled buffers, and degraded the flow that remained deliverable. LFA does not show this behavior because it discards packets it cannot route. Bounding such floods, for example with the hop-count limit used in our router firmware, is a necessary refinement. In preparing these results we also found and corrected a defect in our simulator's handling of flooded acknowledgements, which were never deduplicated and could circulate until buffers overflowed; before the correction, TinyNet failed to recover in 11 of the 213 flows. All TinyNet results in this paper use the corrected simulator.}

Key observations from these results include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rapid Recovery from Failures:} \textcolor{red}{After one to four simultaneous node failures, TinyNet's median blackout is 1.2--3.9\,ms and its median loss is at most 13 packets per flow, with no knowledge of the topology and no pre-computed alternates.}
    \item \textbf{Impact of Multiple Node Failures:} \textcolor{red}{Loss and blackout grow only modestly with the number of failures, whereas an LFA network typically waits for full reconvergence once more than one node fails.}
    \item \textbf{Capacity for Message Maintenance in Failures:} TinyNet sustains message transmission even under substantial network failures. In contrast to spanning-tree protocols, which lose connectivity to entire sub-trees on a single node failure~\cite{spb}, TinyNet reroutes around failed nodes by flooding. \textcolor{red}{The same flooding is a liability when failures partition the network: packets for an unreachable destination can congest the surviving network, which caused the one non-recovery in our experiment.}
\end{itemize}
